Czech VAT liability for your supplier's unpaid VAT (§109)

Updated 21 October 2026 · about 6 minutes

In the Czech Republic a VAT payer that buys from another Czech VAT payer can become guarantor (ručitel) for the VAT that the supplier fails to pay. The tax office can then ask the buyer to pay that VAT a second time. The rules are in §109 of Act No. 235/2004 Sb., on value added tax (ZDPH). This guide explains the situations that trigger it and the simple checks that reduce the risk.

Who this applies to

§109 applies to a VAT payer (plátce DPH) that receives a taxable supply with place of supply in the Czech Republic from another VAT payer, or pays for one. That includes foreign companies that are registered for Czech VAT. If you are not registered for Czech VAT, see our guide for foreign companies.

The general rule: "knew or should have known"

Under §109(1) the buyer is liable for the supplier's unpaid VAT if, when the tax became due, it knew or should and could have known that the VAT would intentionally not be paid. §109(2) then lists situations in which the law presumes that the buyer knew. These presumptions are what matters day to day.

The supplier is published as an unreliable VAT payer (nespolehlivý plátce)The supplier's status in the VAT register on the date of supply and on the date you pay

No minimum amount for unpublished bank accounts since 2025

Older guides say the unpublished-account rule only applies to payments above twice the cash-payment limit (700,000 Kč, later 540,000 Kč). That threshold no longer applies. The text of §109(2)(c)(2) in force since 1 January 2025 contains no amount: any bank transfer, wholly or partly, to an account that is not published for the supplier triggers the presumption. In practice this means checking the account for every payment, including small ones. Accounts held abroad are not published in the Czech register, so payments to them fall under the same rule.

What the courts have said

Both decisions below concern the unreliable-payer rule before the 2025 amendment, when it sat in §109(3), and they pull in different directions:

  • Supreme Administrative Court, 5 Afs 60/2021 (2022): a VAT payer that received a supply from a supplier listed as an unreliable payer was liable for the supplier's unpaid VAT, and "no further condition" had to be met for the liability to arise. Decision text
  • Supreme Administrative Court, 3 Afs 114/2018 (2021): the court annulled a guarantor assessment and held that, under EU law, the published unreliable status alone should not automatically make the buyer liable; the circumstances of the case had to be assessed. Decision text

Since 2025 the law itself frames the unreliable status as a presumption of knowledge. Either way, the outcome depends on facts the buyer can control: whether it checked the supplier and when. A dated record of your checks is useful evidence.

How to protect yourself

  1. Check the supplier's status before each supply and each payment in the VAT register (see how to check a Czech supplier).
  2. Pay only to an account published for that supplier. If the invoice shows a different account, ask the supplier to publish it first.
  3. Use the special method of securing tax (§109a ZDPH) if you have doubts: you may pay the VAT amount directly to the supplier's tax office instead of to the supplier, and the supplier receives only the price without VAT.
  4. Keep evidence of each check (date, result). Supplier Watch keeps a daily log for every supplier you watch.
